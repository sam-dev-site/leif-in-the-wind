## Ecoregion

- Real-world analogue: the outer coast of the **Olympic Peninsula**, Washington, about 48°N. The west-facing shore and the coastal forest behind it, from the beaches (Kalaloch, Ruby Beach, the trails out of Ozette) into the Sitka spruce forest. The Hoh, Queets, Quinault, and Bogachiel valleys are the rain-forest face of the same edge.
- Sitka spruce grows in a narrow strip along the north Pacific, from south-central Alaska to northern California. On the west side of the Olympic Peninsula that strip widens onto the coastal plain. Kew lists it as native to Alaska, British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, and California, and as introduced in Newfoundland. It is not the spruce behind the Vinland strand.
- Compressed journey: in the game this band sits inland of the Connecticut. The walk is shortened. The miles from New England to this ocean are not tiled. On the real map this is the Pacific shore of the later United States. The chunk order is the mission’s ladder, not a longitude sort.
- Not the California redwood coast. That forest is real, and it is a shorter southern strip. This pass takes the Sitka spruce shore, which is the long Pacific edge.
- Not the Columbia as another stem to cross. The win is the shore, not one more river.
- Not Haida Gwaii and not the Newfoundland coast wearing a new name. The landing already has balsam fir and boreal spruce. This shore has different trees.
- Period: 11th century. The spruce, hemlock, and cedar were already the old shore. The park, the boardwalks, and the logged-out valleys are later. The shore was already lived in. Makah and Quileute records are why the berries are food. This file does not design a meeting, a village, or a hunt.

## Climate

Wet maritime. The Olympic rain-forest valleys get about 140 to 167 inches of rain a year. Storms off the Pacific drop it on the west-facing side. Low-valley temperatures seldom drop below freezing, and summer highs rarely reach 80°F. The game’s night stays the coast rule anyway: same cold, same hearts. Do not invent a warm night, a rain that spends hearts, or a fog clock.

Ground underfoot, in two kinds:

- **The shore.** Where the forest gives way to the ocean. Wind-flagged trees, drift logs thrown up from the rivers. This is the west edge. He wins by standing on the walkable ground there. He does not win by walking into the water.
- **The coastal forest.** Sitka spruce, western hemlock, and western redcedar, with salmonberry and salal underneath. This is the walk behind that edge.

A few wet prairies break the forest. They were kept open by burning. They are not this band’s face, and they are not the Rice Lake grass.

No granite Vinland strand, no peat bog as the look, no cypress, no chestnut terrace.

## Do not include

- **Balsam fir, boreal spruce, tamarack, tuckamore, bakeapple, blueberry, peat, and granite as this shore’s face.** Those are the landing. Sitka spruce has been planted in Newfoundland. That does not make it native there.
- **Eastern hemlock, sugar maple, American chestnut, eastern cottonwood, baldcypress, water tupelo.** Earlier bands, and out of range on this coast.
- **Coast redwood as this band.** A different Pacific forest.
- **Douglas-fir as the tree he sees first.** It belongs more to the drier, burned stands than to the outer coastal forest.
- **A bracken prairie as the band.** The open prairies were kept by burning. They are not the Rice Lake grass and not the win.
- **A pitch drop from Sitka spruce.** The coast pitch stack is balsam fir and the Newfoundland spruce. This chop pays wood.
- **A salmon catch, a canoe, a whale, or seal oil.** The berry’s name is not a fishery. The oil in the food notes is not a craft.
- **An elk path or a deer path.** Roosevelt elk are here. The valley already has the deer. This band does not add a trail.
- **A village, a potlatch, or a person who hands him the win.** The story is the walk. The line is the shore.
