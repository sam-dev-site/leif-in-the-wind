# Biome research: `river_valley`

Status: **draft — owner should skim before art or spawn tables**

Research only. Nothing in this file is authorized for `game/`, spawn tables, art filenames, or PixelLab prompts until that skim.

This is the first biome change underfoot: temperate deciduous river country, clay under the valley, a river you cannot walk through. Slice 0 stays `coastal_north`. The plants named below are the ones `coastal_north` saved for inland.

## Ecoregion

- Real-world analogue: the **Saint John River valley** and the **inner Southwest Miramichi**, New Brunswick, about 46–47°N. Wallace puts the saga camp Hóp in the Miramichi–Chaleur country because butternuts, riverbank grapes, barrier sandbars, and a long-occupied Indigenous shore coincide there. The Saint John is where those same hardwoods are actually common, on a floodplain you cannot ford.
- Compressed journey: in the game this valley sits inland of the Vinland boreal coast. The walk is shortened. The species are not.
- The St. Lawrence valley west of Baie-Saint-Paul is also inside the butternut line. It is the same kind of country, not a separate biome and not a reason to drag in the whole eastern seaboard.
- Period: 11th century. Prefer species present then. Butternut canker and hemlock woolly adelgid are modern. Today’s sick or rare stands are not the 11th-century forest.
- The valley was already lived in. On the Saint John the people are the **Wolastoqiyik** (Wəlastəkwewiyik, “people of the beautiful river”; Maliseet in older books); the river is the **Wəlastəkw**. On the Miramichi, **Mi’kmaq** ancestors have been there for at least three thousand years. This file does not design meetings, villages, or combat.

## Climate

Sheltered temperate-deciduous valley. The Saint John River Valley ecoregion is warmer than the highlands around it: mean annual temperature about 4.5°C, summer about 15°C, winter about −5°C, precipitation about 1000–1200 mm. Warm moist summers, snowy winters. The Grand Lake lowlands are the warmest pocket, with the longest growing season and the most southern trees.

Not the coastal recipe. Granite, peat, tuckamore, and cold maritime bog are the shore. Here the ground is river valley:

- Under Fredericton the buried valley holds up to about 60 m of glacial and post-glacial fill, including a buried estuarine **clay-silt**. Earlier maps called that water glacial Lake Acadia. The 2013 borehole study reads the unit as freshwater to brackish, from meltwater and marine incursion during deglaciation.
- The surface of the intervale — the ground you would walk — is **silt and fine sand**, laid on again every spring freshet. Slopes carry loam to **clay loam**, and Stobbe describes boulder clay on the valley slopes, often under a few feet of sand.
- Game language “clay” means that valley fill and those clay loams. Do not paint every tile as bare blue clay. The floodplain skin is silt. The 11th-century cover is forest. Farms and orchards on these soils are post-settlement clearing (a quarter of the ecoregion is farmland now). Do not spawn fields.

**The river is not a brook.** The Saint John runs about 675 km and is the largest river of the Maritime peninsula. At Fredericton the bottom is, in places, deeper than 5.5 m. Every spring the freshet covers the intervale. The Miramichi is a long river with a mouth miles wide; ships in the saga description get over the estuary bars only at high tide. Shoals at a mouth are not a ford. Beaver dams belong on tributaries. They do not turn the main stem into a pond you walk around. Travel tools for this biome are a raft or a timber bridge. Brooks can still be waded.

## Flora (must have sources)

Hardwoods are the change from fir and spruce. Hemlock and white pine are the conifers of this valley. Fir and spruce still occur; they are not the canopy to chop.

Wood carried to L’Anse aux Meadows is the archaeological link, not the whole flora. A butternut burl and three butternuts were in the Norse carpentry waste. Butternut has never grown in Newfoundland, including during the medieval warm period. Wallace’s lecture also lists scraps of **linden, beech, red ash, and elm**. The Canadian Encyclopedia article (Wallace) adds wood of **eastern hemlock**, and spells linden *Thilia* — the genus is *Tilia*. Red ash in that older usage is green ash (`Fraxinus pennsylvanica`).

## Fauna (must have sources)

The island rules do not travel. Moose are native here. The hare is the snowshoe hare. White-tailed deer are native and are the intervale trail, and they were not the dominant animal of the closed mature forest.

## Do not include

Wrong for this valley in the 11th century, or not established by this pass:

- **Coyote** (`Canis latrans`) — a recent immigrant; first common in New Brunswick around 1973. The ecozone’s modern mammal list includes it. That listing is not an 11th-century animal.
- **Arctic hare** — Newfoundland’s native hare. Here the hare is the snowshoe hare.
- **Moose as an introduction** — that ban is only for insular Newfoundland. Moose belong in this file.
- **Caribou trails** — caribou were in the province; they are not the path through this hardwood valley. Coastal North already owns that trail.
- **Elk and bison** — not in the Saint John or Miramichi sources used here. They stay in `inland_plains` until that file is researched.
- **Giant beaver** — tradition, not `Castor canadensis`.
- **Orchard apple, domestic grape (`Vitis vinifera`), fox grape as a native vine** — riverbank grape is the species. Orchards in the ecozone description are modern farmland.
- **“Self-sown wheat” or barley as a named plant** — the sagas say Hóp had self-sown wheat. These sources do not identify that plant. Our Landscape Heritage mentions wild rice in a marsh at Pickerel Pond and does not name the species or connect it to the saga. Do not spawn a grain from the saga line, and do not treat that one marsh as valley-wide forage.
- **Maize and tobacco as wild forage** — O’Donnell reports no Miramichi Mi’kmaq agriculture except tobacco. Gardens are a separate research pass, not scatter-spawn.
- **Bakeapple, peat, granite, tuckamore** as the look of this ground — that is `coastal_north`.
- **Balsam fir and spruce as the default chop** — they occur; the signature trees are the hardwoods, hemlock, and white pine.
- **Butternut canker and hemlock woolly adelgid** as the 11th-century look. Both are modern.
- **Wild turkey, eastern cougar, passenger pigeon, red squirrel, eastern chipmunk** — not established by the sources read for this pass. Do not add them to fill space, and do not copy Newfoundland’s “introduced squirrel and chipmunk” ban onto this mainland without a source.
- Palms and generic fantasy berries.

## How this helps Leif go inland

The spruce shore does not contain the grapes or the butternuts. This valley does, and a river sits across the trail. Food, timber, and a crossing are how the walk continues. The camp on the coast is still not the destination.
